Risk & controls · Fictional example

When a procedure exists, but isn’t followed.

A written procedure tells us what should happen. To understand whether it works, we also need evidence of what happens in practice.

Consider an approval control

Imagine a procedure requiring independent approval before payment. A review finds that approval was recorded after several payments had already been made.

Ask two different questions

Design: Is the control clearly defined, assigned to an appropriate person and capable of addressing the risk? Are absence cover and exceptions addressed?

Operation: Is the control being performed consistently, at the required time, with evidence retained?

Understand the cause

Speak with colleagues and examine the workflow. The issue might involve unclear responsibilities, unavailable approvers, system limitations or a control that is routinely bypassed. Establish the cause rather than assuming it.

Make the response fit

Clarify responsibilities, improve the process or reinforce an existing requirement according to the evidence. Then check whether the change works.
